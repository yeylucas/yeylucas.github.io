Driving the Future: Self-Driving Cars and Public Trust

Lucas Yeykelis · 22 April 2024

A note from the present: I wrote this as my senior thesis in high school, and it's pretty cool to see how relevant this still is today.

“Wait until you tell your car to take you to someplace the Man thinks you shouldn’t be going, and it replies, ‘I’m sorry, Dave, I’m afraid I can’t do that’” (Butler).

Luckily, when I went on a self-driving car ride, the only thing the car told me was to buckle my seatbelt. As I smoothly traversed the steep San Francisco streets in a self-driving Waymo car, it was hard to believe that I was being driven by a machine. Artificial intelligence (AI), which is the brains behind self-driving cars, has proved to be one of the most influential technological advancements in human history. According to the Pew Research Center, nearly every person who uses modern applications interacts with some form of AI whether they know it or not (Kennedy). Another such advancement was the invention of the automobile, completely transforming transportation and in turn structuring the society we know today. Further, like many other domains, the car industry has integrated AI systems, most notably resulting in self-driving vehicles (SDVs). The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) emphasizes that SDVs could “increase mobility” and “expand transportation options” while having the ability to increase safety (“Automated”). Since self-driving cars cannot get drunk, text while driving, or drive above the speed limit, the main causes of injury or damage can be significantly reduced, leading to safer driving conditions.

Although SDVs can provide increased safety and mobility, people are reluctant to wave these cars through and give them the green light, not only literally but figuratively. The moment I opened the door to the Waymo, I felt like a celebrity as people would take out their phones and record my interaction. However, even though the public seems to be in awe that a phantom driver is behind the wheel of a car that shares the same road as them, the reactions are not all positive. In a 2024 survey by the American Automobile Association (AAA), “most U.S. drivers either express fear (66%) or uncertainty (25%) about fully self-driving vehicles,” and “‘[t]here has been an increase in consumer fear over the past few years,’ said Greg Brannon, the director of automotive engineering research for AAA” (Moye).

With the rollout of self-driving cars on our public roads, we must address the problem of a lack of public trust towards the deployment of self-driving vehicles. We should re-evaluate the intended use cases of these cars and prioritize context-specific integration that fosters inclusivity and transparency for users (Othman; Rodrigo; Bastola; Schulke).

Background: What Are Self-Driving Cars and How Do They Work?

Autonomous vehicles, like the self-driving cars that roam the streets of San Francisco, are enabled by AI technology that completely controls the vehicle. SDVs have lidar, radar, and infrared sensors in addition to numerous cameras and other features to provide a sense of its environment. The information from these devices are then fed as input to a combination of neural networks which drive its decision-making process in real time. Since these networks are trained to be a safe driver, it can never go above the speed limit or weave between traffic, removing the element of human error. However, the prediction and correct decision-making that is made by the car improves with more data that the network can be trained on as the model trains on more edge cases. Because driving has almost infinite possibilities to account for, it is difficult to create a self-driving car that can act perfectly in every scenario. A recent example of this was discussed in a CNN article where two independent Waymo cars hit the same tow truck towing a pickup truck. According to Waymo, “Both Waymo cars incorrectly interpreted what their cameras were seeing and, because of that, wrongly predicted how the truck was going to move” (Valdes-Dapena).

The landscape of self-driving cars in the United States consists of a few non-exhaustive key players: Waymo (a subsidiary of Google), Zoox (a subsidiary of Amazon), Aurora, and Kodiak. These are the main companies that have developed full self-driving vehicles and deployed them onto public roads in the United States. Although the self-driving cars we are accustomed to seeing are robotaxis like Waymo and Zoox, Aurora and Kodiak focus on the trucking industry. Trucking is vital to the United States’ economy, as “Seventy-two percent of goods in America are shipped by truck, and in most communities, trucks are the only form of delivery” (“Fact Sheet”). However, long-haul truck driving consists of monotonous highway driving and is considered an unfavorable job by many due to the long working hours away from home (“Fact Sheet”). With self-driving trucks, not only will this allow for increased safety, but increased efficiency, as freight can be hauled without the need for human breaks to sleep or use the bathroom. Additionally, self-driving technology limited to highway driving is generally easier to implement compared to robotaxis that have to maneuver stimulating streets and account for pedestrians and bikes.

Benefits: Improved Safety, Accessibility, Environmental Quality, and Efficiency

The most obvious benefit of self-driving cars are its safety improvements. In an article by the Center for Sustainable Systems from the University of Michigan, it is noted that “In 2021, U.S. annual vehicular fatality rate was 42,915; 94% of crashes are due to human error. AVs have the potential to remove/reduce human error and decrease deaths. AVs have the potential to reduce crashes by 90%, potentially saving approximately $190 billion per year” (“Autonomous Vehicles Factsheet”). Two years earlier in 2019 alone, the NHTSA reports that car crashes cost America $340 billion, with the leading factors behind this being alcohol, distracted driving, and speeding (NHTSA). It is clear that self-driving cars can remove human impairment if trained and implemented to scale, but there is also the indirect benefit of an overall safer environment which can create some peace of mind and enhance public health.

Besides being safer, self-driving cars can offer disabled persons or those with limited mobility greater accessibility. According to the National Disability Institute, autonomous vehicles can not only provide greater transportation opportunities, but also allow for an improved “quality of life and mental well being” (Modicamore). Self-driving cars have the ability to give disabled people more independence, making it easier to go to the doctor, get out of the home, and socialize with others (Modicamore).

Lastly, self-driving cars can be more efficient and therefore are more environmentally friendly. Because acceleration and speed is more controlled and stable, the U.S. Department of Energy describes that “this driving style – which industry experts refer to as automated ‘eco-driving’ – can reduce fuel consumption by 15 to 20%” (qtd. in Igini). If there are multiple SDVs on the road, they can cooperate through potential car-to-car communication and drive even more efficiently, avoiding unnecessary stops and maintaining a constant speed. Despite these benefits, self-driving cars have been met with resistance from the public.

Problems: Public Trust Issues and Resistance Towards Self-Driving Cars

Many people have been expressing their concerns regarding self-driving cars and its implications such as the removal of jobs, overall safety, and the centralized and controllable form of transportation. In a Pew Research Center study on public perception of self-driving cars, it is found that “Roughly six-in-ten adults (63%) [said] they would not want to ride in a driverless passenger vehicle if they had the opportunity” (Rainie). Since the foundational motives for self-driving cars can allow for increased accessibility and greater efficiency, we must face these aforementioned concerns now before more issues arise during large-scale implementation. In a 2023 study by Arne Schulke, the professor of Management Accounting and Supply Chain Management at IUBH, he corroborates that “[e]ven though most participants perceived SDVs to drive better than humans, thus increasing road safety, 67.2 percent did not want to share the road with them. This raises severe challenges for policy makers towards public acceptance of their wide-scale introduction” (Schulke). Although participants understand that self-driving cars can significantly reduce accidents resulting in human error, they are reluctant to trust these cars even when people’s “interest in advanced driver assistance systems remains high” (Schulke; Moye). It is possible that the general public does not have a great understanding of self-driving cars and how they work since there is almost no advertising. Those with more formal education and who have heard more about self-driving cars are more likely to accept or support self-driving cars compared to others (Kennedy).

Although SDVs can be safer than human drivers, accidents are bound to happen. In 2023, as described in a Washington Post article, a Cruise self-driving car in San Francisco dragged a woman “about 20 feet” and Cruise subsequently got their public driving permit revoked (Thadani). Although the woman was initially hit by a human driver, she was “flung” in front of a Cruise vehicle (Thadani). At first, the car did its job by applying the brakes, but a short moment later, it started to move to the side of the road and further dragged the woman; aggravating her injuries (Thadani). This incident received a lot of media attention and further decreased the public’s trust of SDVs, even when most self-driving cars have only been in minor accidents.

Relating to one of the trust issues from the public, self-driving cars can in some ways restrict freedoms even when they are meant to enhance them. We see similarities for many technologies such as social media, where the main goal is to bring people together, however as we know social media may ironically have the opposite effect. In an opinion piece from the National Review, a news publication, George Will emphasizes that “[t]he automobile encourages people in delusions of adequacy, which make them resistant to government by experts who know what choices people should make” (qtd. in Butler). In essence, the author believes that self-driving cars can strip people of their individualism because “[a] car, used properly, is a tremendous source of freedom—and a tremendous responsibility” (Butler). The idea of paving one’s way in the pursuit of the American Dream is partly influenced by automobiles and the ability to explore, which is a fundamental part of American culture. If private cars were to be fully self-driving without manual driving capabilities, our means of transportation could possibly be controlled by the respective company. Further, if robotaxis were to be implemented at a grand scale, the loss of cabbies can lead to a lack of human interaction (“Cherish Your”). If we were to spiral deeper, a lack of human interaction can also lead to loneliness, which should be taken into account when implementing and designing SDVs. Ultimately, the effects of self-driving cars boils down to its specific integrations to society, whether these cars be sold for private use, used for taxis, or for automating long-haul truck driving.

If self-driving cars were to be implemented for robotaxi or truck driving purposes, it is at the expense of taxi drivers and truck drivers. Todd Spencer, the president of the Owner-Operator Independent Drivers Association, states that truckers “know they do an important job in our society, and they would prefer not to see their job eliminated via technology” (qtd. in Rodrigo). Although job loss concerns are valid, a study funded by the Department of Transportation shows that layoffs for long-haul truck drivers will not be significant and that there will actually be an “Increase [in] total U.S. employment by 26,400 to 35,100 jobs per year on average” (Waschik). Whether the numbers lie or not, it is true that there is anxiety amongst those who could be impacted by the implementation of SDVs, suggesting that there is a lack of communication between industry leaders and the public. This only detriments the trust between the public and self-driving car companies and can contribute to the overall disapproval of SDVs. Unfortunately, it is not clear whether SDVs will be limited to certain use cases or for potentially private use, leading to uncertainty and speculation.

Solutions: Context-Specific Integration & Inclusive and Transparent Design

Taking this into consideration, possible solutions include finding a specific realm in which self-driving cars can be integrated and subsequently develop inclusive and transparent design choices. Most people fear self-driving cars because they fear the unknown, as these vehicles are still being tested and it is difficult for most people to imagine a tangible scenario of these cars being used. As discussed earlier, one specific example is to retrofit self-driving technologies on trucks that can transport freight. The Washington Post discusses the significance of self-driving trucks and describes how it can “‘change the geography of our economy in the way that railroads and shipping did,’” according to “Steve Viscelli, a sociologist at the University of Pennsylvania who studies the trucking industry” (Thadani).

To address the concerns from truck drivers, industry leaders should engage in “‘really honest conversations,’” as denoted by Andrew Culhane, the chief strategy officer for a self-driving truck company called Torc (qtd. in Rodrigo). It is often that companies try to put out technologies in a rush, hoping to get a headstart on the opportunities that they can claim. However, this approach is not sustainable and can even be dangerous if technology is released too fast without proper safety assessments or testing. Further, the arguments relating to job loss are partly aggravated by the lack of legislation and active self-driving trucks on the road that can serve as a real-world reference. In an article from the National Library of Medicine, it is stated that “the risk on truck drivers is real, but the projections touted are overstated because companies will not be able to abandon all drivers as drivers do more than just driving and not all the tasks can be automated” (Othman). Relating back to the theme of uncertainty, “The broader implications of AVs will be dependent on how the technology is adopted in various transportation systems,” which is “significantly determined” by regulations and laws that can draw the line for these technologies (Othman). As of late, most implementations of self-driving cars have been either for testing or robotaxi purposes, especially in the San Francisco Bay Area, which provide a very limited illustration of its potential. Should there be more clarification or concrete regulation as to how self-driving cars can be integrated into society, this could lead to a greater trust and understanding of self-driving cars. Because of a lack of regulation, it makes sense why so many truck drivers and other people affected by these technologies are wary of SDVs. A starting point, however, could be a compromise between industry leaders and truck drivers where they can find a middle ground by noting what exactly will be automated (Rodrigo). For instance, long highway driving could be automated until the last mile, and then when the vehicle needs to drive within cities or to facilities, this action may be taken over by human drivers (Rodrigo).

On the other hand, inclusive and transparent designing of the self-driving car experience can allow for more acceptance of these cars due to the increased accessibility and overall greater understanding of the vehicle as a result of implementing these design choices. In a paper by Ashish Bastola and Julian Brinkley from the Clemson University School of Computing, the authors emphasize the importance of design in SDVs and how this is crucial for gaining and retaining users (Bastola). Since not all individuals have the same preferences or needs, individualized profiles that could be created via an app should be transferred to the riding experience, especially when interacting with mobility as a service (Bastola). One of the reasons why disabled individuals do not trust self-driving cars is because of a lack of understanding of the technicalities or inner workings of the vehicle, and their valid distrust for these cars are heightened because of their increased vulnerabilities (Vijaygopal). For a more reassuring ride, displays within the car should describe or illustrate why the car made a decision. With this additional explainability, the user can gain more trust of the vehicle and worry less about every action it makes.

Other measures to improve the public’s trust and perception of SDVs can simply be to create more exposure. It was previously mentioned that people who have heard more about SDVs and those who have more formal education are more likely to accept SDVs, which means that the average person most likely does not have a comprehensive understanding of these cars (Rainie). Because there is very little legislation relating to SDVs, it is difficult to lay out specific solutions to problems that could change depending on the specific use cases for SDVs. For example, standardization of in-vehicle experiences could allow for people to become adjusted to this technology quicker and reduce confusion (Bastola). To address SDVs as a whole, more marketing and advertising should expose individuals to the benefits and use cases of these cars and reduce the untrusting stigma around SDVs (Vijaygopal).

Conclusion: The Future of Self-Driving Cars

Self-driving cars are still in their early stages of development, and because of their infancy, the public is going to take some time to figure them out as well as industry leaders and government officials. At this moment in time, SDVs could very well be at their lowest point in terms of usage and acceptance compared to the foreseeable future. Due to the lack of laws and rules for SDVs, regulations play a key role in determining the specific integration for these cars. With more legislation, companies can design and plan according to the rules put in place to create the most optimal experience for users. As described in a Washington Post article, “Aurora and Kodiak both support the idea of federal regulation, which would give them more certainty about standards as they expand nationally” (Thadani). Daniel Goff, the head of policy at Kodiak, further mentions that “Having a federal framework gives confidence to regulators and the public,” giving the public the much needed reassurance that SDVs are being thoughtfully developed (Thadani).

As exciting as self-driving cars can be, we must carefully consider specific use cases for these cars and create adequate legislation that can guide mindful design and engineering principles. With this, the public can come to a better understanding of SDVs and resultantly build trust in these cars; revealing the benefits that SDVs behold more quickly and easily. On the road to a safer and more accessible world, there will be roadblocks along the way, but we cannot let them impede the final destination.
